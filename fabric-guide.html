The Fabric Guide

What every common fibre does well, what it hates, and how to choose between them before you reach the till.

Clothes are cloth first. Cut and finishing matter, but the fibre decides how a garment feels on a hot afternoon, how it behaves after twenty washes and whether it still looks like itself in three years. This guide covers the fibres you will actually meet on a label.

Cotton

Cotton is the default for a reason: it breathes, it takes dye well, it is comfortable against skin and it costs little. It also creases, shrinks in its first hot wash and takes a long time to dry. Long-staple cottons such as Supima and Egyptian are smoother and stronger because the individual fibres are longer, so fewer ends poke out of the yarn.

Linen

Linen is spun from flax and is the coolest fibre you can wear in heat, because the yarn is stiff enough to hold the cloth slightly away from the skin. It creases immediately and that is simply what linen does. It gets softer and more absorbent every wash, so it rewards patience more than almost anything else in a wardrobe.

Wool and cashmere

Wool manages moisture better than any synthetic: it absorbs up to a third of its weight in water vapour before it feels damp, which is why it stays comfortable in changeable weather. Merino is fine enough to wear next to skin. Cashmere is finer and warmer still but weaker, so it pills and needs folding rather than hanging.

Silk

Silk is strong for its weight, drapes beautifully and feels cool at first touch. It is also the least forgiving fibre on this list: perspiration, deodorant and sunlight all mark it permanently. Treat silk as something to wear often and wash gently, not something to store away.

Viscose, modal and lyocell

These are made by dissolving wood pulp and re-forming it as fibre, so they sit between natural and synthetic. They drape like silk and breathe reasonably well. Viscose is noticeably weak when wet, so it should never be wrung or hung dripping. Lyocell is the strongest of the three and handles the washing machine far better.

Polyester, nylon and elastane

Synthetics resist creasing, dry fast and hold their shape. Polyester is the workhorse; nylon is tougher and more abrasion resistant; elastane, added at two to five per cent, is what lets a garment stretch and spring back. Their weakness is breathability and odour: they hold smells that wash out of wool and cotton easily.

Weave, knit and weight

Two garments in the same fibre can behave completely differently. A woven cloth has threads at right angles and barely stretches; a knit is made of loops and moves in every direction. Weight is given in GSM, grams per square metre. A summer shirt sits around 110–140 GSM, a everyday T-shirt 150–200 GSM, a winter sweatshirt 300 GSM and up.

How to use this in a shop

Find the composition label before you look at the price. Rub the cloth between finger and thumb and watch how it recovers. Scrunch a corner in your fist for five seconds and see what the creases do. Hold it up to the light to judge how closely it is woven. None of this takes longer than a minute, and it tells you more than any marketing copy.
